Introduction

Inflation is a fall in the value of money, not a rise in prices

Those two sentences describe the same event from opposite ends. It is worth starting with the second one, because it is the one people feel.

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over a period, usually a year. If inflation is 10%, a basket of goods that cost 100,000 soum last December costs 110,000 soum this December.

Purchasing power is the other side of the same coin: how much a fixed amount of money actually buys. If prices rise 10% and your salary does not, the number on your payslip is unchanged but it commands about 9% fewer goods. The payslip number is nominal. What it buys is real. Almost every argument in economics about inflation comes down to keeping those two apart.

Statisticians measure this with the Consumer Price Index (CPI): fix a basket of what a typical household buys — bread, rice, meat, rent, transport, gas, electricity — price the same basket every month, and report how much the total has moved. The headline figure quoted in this dashboard is the December-on-December change, the measure the Central Bank of Uzbekistan targets.

In plain words

Say this: “Inflation does not make things better or worse in themselves. It shrinks the ruler we measure value with. A million soum in 2017 and a million soum in 2025 are the same number and not the same thing — the calculator below shows you exactly how different.”

Consumer prices

Headline inflation, year by year

This is the most quoted number in Uzbek economic news: how much the whole consumer basket rose over twelve months.

Three things stand out. The spike to in 2017, the year the soum was floated and imported goods repriced. The long, uneven decline since 2019. And in 2025 — the lowest reading in nine years, though still above the Central Bank's 5% target.

In plain words

Say this: “Every bar is one year, not a running total. Even the shortest bar means prices went up. Inflation falling from 15% to 7% does not mean things got cheaper — it means they got more expensive, more slowly.”

If asked about 2017: the Statistics Agency originally published 14.4%. The Central Bank and the IMF now carry 18.8%, which fully counts the pass-through from the float. This dashboard uses the revised figure and says so in the sources.

Is 7% high? Compared with the neighbours

A number is only high relative to something. Against the 5% target, is still too high. Against the region it looks different: in 2025 Uzbekistan's inflation was below Kazakhstan's and Kyrgyzstan's, and it has followed the same broad shape as its neighbours — a 2022 spike after the invasion of Ukraine, then disinflation. Uzbekistan's problem was never a unique one; it was a higher starting level after 2017.

Food, goods and services

The kind of inflation changed, not just the amount

The headline number hides what is getting more expensive. Splitting the basket into food, other goods and services tells a different story for each year.

In 2022 food led: against a headline of 12.3%, the post-invasion spike in grain, oil and fertiliser. By 2024 the picture had inverted. Food rose only — a good harvest and falling vegetable prices — while services rose , driven by the energy-tariff reform in the next section. That is the same headline range, from a completely different cause.

Why it matters: food is a much larger share of a poor household's budget than a rich one's, so food-led inflation is regressive. Tariff-led inflation hits everyone who heats a home, which is everyone — but its weight in the official basket is smaller than its weight in a winter bill.

So whose inflation is the headline figure? Nobody's in particular — it is the average household's. The weights it uses are not published beside the group figures, but four years of both sides is enough to recover them by least squares, and the fit below reproduces every published headline to within 0.02 points. Once you have the weights you can replace them with your own.

In plain words

Say this: “There is no single inflation rate. There is one for each household, depending on what it buys. In 2022 the people hurt most were those who spend most on food. In 2024 it was anyone paying a gas bill. Same headline, different victims.”

Fuel and energy

Energy is the price that moves every other price

Petrol, gas and electricity matter far beyond the bill. Everything in the shop arrived on a lorry and was made in a heated building.

Economists call this a cost-push effect: energy is an input to almost every good, so a rise passes through into food, fares and construction with a lag of a few months. For decades Uzbekistan held energy prices below cost by decree. Removing those subsidies is economically sound — and is the single clearest driver of the 2024 services number.

In plain words

Say this: “Cheap energy was never free. It was paid for out of the budget, by everyone, invisibly. Raising tariffs made the cost visible and put it on the people who use the most. That is fairer — and it is also why 2024 inflation was almost entirely a services story.”

If asked why petrol is shown as steps rather than a line: the price was administered, so a line between decisions would imply movement that never happened. Only documented changes are plotted.

Exchange rate

The soum against the dollar

On 5 September 2017 Uzbekistan floated its currency. The official rate went from to soum per dollar overnight.

Before the float there were two rates: an official one and a much weaker parallel-market one. The reform did not destroy value — it revealed a weakness that was already there, and let trade and investment run on honest numbers. For an economy that imports machinery, cars, medicine and much of its manufactured goods, a weaker soum feeds directly into shop prices: imported inflation. That is most of the 2017 spike.

Then something rarely mentioned: in 2025 the soum strengthened, from to per dollar — the first annual appreciation since the float. Record gold prices, strong remittances and a positive real interest rate (section 08) pulled in the same direction.

In plain words

Say this: “2017 is not a crash. It is a correction. The old rate was a price the government set, not one the market would pay. And notice the last point — in 2025 the line turns down for the first time. A currency that strengthens is a currency people want to hold, and that is the best evidence that disinflation is real.”

Comparison

All of it in one picture

Prices and exchange rates are measured in different units. You cannot put them on one chart as they are — and you must never put them on two vertical axes.

The honest fix is indexing: set every series to 100 in the same base year — here 2017, the float year — and plot how far each has travelled since. Now they share one scale and one axis, and the only thing being compared is relative change. A value of 200 means “twice its 2017 level”. Both series below are end-of-year values, so their timing matches too.

The same trick answers the regional question properly. Annual rates (Figure 2) show who had the worst year; compounding them shows who has had the worst decade.

In plain words

Say this: “This is the one methodological rule in the whole talk. Two different units on two different axes produce a correlation that is an accident of where you put the scales. Index to a common base instead, and the comparison becomes real — and the timing has to match, which is why both lines here are end-of-year values.”

Forecast

A simple forecast, tested against its own past

A forecast is an assumption made visible. This section states the assumption in one line, lets you move it, and then checks how well it would have done.

The model has one idea: inflation is persistent, but it is pulled back towards an anchor each year, closing a fixed fraction of the remaining gap:

next year = anchor + persistence × (this year − anchor)

Persistence is how sticky inflation is: at 0 it snaps to the anchor immediately, at 1 it never converges. The dashed grey line is the IMF's own projection from the World Economic Outlook, so the toy model can be judged against a professional one. The shaded band is not a guess — its width is the model's measured error from the backtest below.

Does it work? Backtesting the model

Any forecast should be asked how it would have done. Here the model stands in each year from 2017 to 2024, is given only that year's actual inflation, and predicts the next. Its mean absolute error is compared with the simplest possible benchmark — “next year equals this year”. The panel updates as you move the sliders above, so you can search for the best parameters by hand, or press one of the buttons to let a grid search do it.

In plain words

Say this: “I am not claiming to know 2029 inflation. Two dials fully determine that line. What I can show is that this one-line model beats the naive guess on the last eight years — points of error against — and that the IMF's path and mine broadly agree.”

Then the finding: “When you let the data choose the anchor, it does not pick 5%. It picks about . For eight years the economy behaved as if its real anchor were around . 2025's is the first reading that clearly breaks below it — which is why it matters more than one number should.”

If asked “why not machine learning?”: ten annual observations is nothing to train on; a model with more parameters than data points fits the past perfectly and the future not at all. The honest upgrade is monthly data (about 110 observations), which would support seasonality and a proper confidence interval.

Economic impact

Inflation moves money between people

Inflation is not only a cost. It is a transfer — it quietly moves purchasing power from one group to another, without anyone voting on it. The data say who lost and who gained.

Lost

Anyone holding cash

A million soum kept under the mattress from 2017 buys of what it did. This is the clearest loss in the whole dashboard, and the calculator measures it exactly.

Lost

Fixed incomes and pensions

Payments set in nominal soum are revalued by decision, not automatically. Every month between revaluations is a real income cut.

Lost

Whoever the year's inflation was aimed at

Food-heavy budgets in 2022; anyone paying a gas bill in 2024. The headline is an average and the average has no victims — the components do.

Gained

People earning a wage

This is the surprise. Nominal wages grew faster than prices every year since 2021: real wages are up about in four years. Inflation moved value from savers to earners.

Gained

Borrowers on fixed rates, exporters

A debt fixed in soum shrank in real terms. Income earned in dollars converted into more soum every year until 2025 — when that stopped.

Mixed

Businesses

Input costs and repricing costs rose and planning got harder; firms that could reprice quickly coped, firms in long contracts absorbed the loss. Disinflation and a stable soum are the relief.

The grid above says savers lost and earners gained. Both claims can be tested with the data already on this page — the next two figures do exactly that.

Did wages keep up?

The question every inflation story should answer and most do not. Nominal wage growth is deflated by annual-average inflation — the correct deflator for an average wage — using real = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, not a simple subtraction. Every year the green bar is positive, the average worker's purchasing power rose.

The policy response, measured properly

The Central Bank's instrument is the policy rate. But a 14% rate means nothing on its own — what matters is the real rate: the policy rate minus inflation. Below zero, borrowing is effectively subsidised and saving in soum is a losing bet; the brake is not on. Above zero, it is. The chart shows the brake was off in 2016–17, on from 2018, and pressed hardest in 2025 at — which is the year inflation fell to its lowest and the soum strengthened. That is not a coincidence.

In plain words

Say this: “Two charts, one story. Real wages rose, so the average worker came out ahead — inflation was a transfer from savers to earners, not a pure loss. And the Bank kept real rates positive for seven years, which is why 2025 finally delivered both low inflation and a stronger soum. Tight money is slow, but it works.”

Conclusion

What the data supports

  1. Inflation is falling and 2025 is the real break. At , the lowest reading in nine years, inflation is below Kazakhstan's and Kyrgyzstan's and, for the first time, below the anchor the economy behaved around since 2018.
  2. The 2017 float explains the level, not the persistence. The float caused a one-off repricing to . What kept inflation in double digits for most of the years that followed was domestic: demand, credit and the slow removal of energy subsidies.
  3. The kind of inflation changed. Food led in 2022 (); services led in 2024 () as gas and electricity tariffs were raised towards cost. Same headline range, opposite victims.
  4. Cash lost, wages won. A million soum held from 2017 keeps of its buying power; the average real wage rose about over 2021–25. Inflation transferred value from savers to earners.
  5. The bank beat the mattress and the dollar. A million soum left in cash from 2017 kept of its buying power; the same money in dollars kept about 64%; in a soum deposit tracking the policy rate it finished roughly 28% ahead of inflation. Soum prices rose faster than the dollar did, so foreign currency was never the safe haven it is assumed to be.
  6. Positive real rates worked, slowly. The real policy rate has been above zero since 2018 and reached in 2025 — the year of the lowest inflation and the first appreciation of the soum since the float.
  7. A forecast is an assumption made visible. A one-line model beats the naive guess on the last eight years and broadly agrees with the IMF's path to 5% by 2027 — but two dials fully determine it, and honest forecasting means showing them.

Closing line

“The soum did not lose value because of one decision in one year. It lost value steadily, for reasons that were visible in the data the whole time — and in 2025, for the first time, the same data show it starting to hold. Measuring it carefully is how you tell the difference.”

Sources & method

Where every number comes from

What is here, and what was left out

Every series on this page was read from the publishing institution or a reputable secondary report on ; the sources below give the exact series, URL and access date. Nothing is estimated. Where a series could not be verified for every year — the group breakdown before 2022, petrol prices between documented decisions — it has been omitted rather than approximated, and the chart says so. All figures live in one file, assets/js/data.js; every chart, calculation and sentence with a number in it reads from there.

Method notes

Revisions. Headline inflation for 2016 and 2017 uses the Central Bank's revised figures (9.7% and 18.8%). The Statistics Agency's original releases showed 5.7% and 14.4% under the pre-reform methodology; the IMF carries 9.8% and 18.8%. Using one institution's series throughout avoids mixing methodologies.

Timing. Headline, group and regional inflation are December-on-December. Exchange rates and the policy rate are year-end values. Wages are January–June averages and are therefore deflated by annual-average inflation, not the Dec/Dec figure. Series with different timing are never placed on the same axis.

Indexing. Series in different units are rebased to 2017 = 100 so they can share one axis. Dual vertical axes are avoided throughout because the correlation they display is an artefact of scale placement.

Purchasing power. The price level compounds Dec/Dec inflation from end-2016 = 100. Real value is the ratio of the two years' price levels. Real wage growth is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.

Forecast and backtest. The model is the conditional mean of a first-order autoregression around a fixed anchor. It is backtested with one-step-ahead forecasts over 2018–2025 against a naive benchmark; parameters are fitted by grid search on mean absolute error; the uncertainty band starts at that error and widens by 35% of it per year. It is an illustration, not an econometric forecast.

Accessibility. Every chart has a data-table alternative, keyboard navigation with the arrow keys, and a palette checked for colour-vision-deficiency separation on both the light and dark surfaces.
